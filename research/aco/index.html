Abstract

Emergency medical response in Kumasi, Ghana is severely constrained by the mismatch between vehicle availability, road conditions, and hospital capability tiers. When ambulances are dispatched by proximity alone, Level-1 trauma cases frequently arrive at under-equipped facilities and require costly secondary transfers that worsen outcomes. This project formulates ambulance dispatch as a multi-objective combinatorial optimization problem on the real Kumasi road network, jointly minimizing expected travel time and maximizing the match between patient trauma severity and hospital capability level.

Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), in its Ant System formulation, is adapted for the multi-objective dispatch setting and benchmarked against four competing methods: Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS), a two-stage stochastic programming formulation that handles demand uncertainty, a deep reinforcement learning policy trained on simulated dispatch scenarios, and a hybrid ACO + machine-learning pipeline in which learned heuristic values replace hand-crafted heuristics in the ACO state-transition rule. The road network is extracted from OpenStreetMap via OSMnx and augmented with hospital capability data.

This work extends my 2023 KNUST undergraduate thesis, which applied ACO to a single-objective version of the same problem. The extension introduces a richer bi-objective formulation, a larger and higher-resolution road graph, and a systematic benchmarking study. Manuscript in preparation.

1 Introduction & Motivation

In a trauma emergency, the first routing decision (which hospital to send the ambulance to) is often the most consequential one. Getting it wrong costs lives.

Kumasi is Ghana's second-largest city and the capital of the Ashanti Region, with a rapidly growing metropolitan population and an emergency medical system under significant pressure. The city's hospital network spans facilities of three capability tiers: Level-3 community health posts with limited trauma equipment, Level-2 district hospitals capable of managing moderate injuries, and Level-1 teaching and specialist hospitals equipped for complex trauma and surgical intervention. The Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH) is the primary Level-1 facility and one of only two major teaching hospitals in the country.

The canonical approach to ambulance dispatch, routing to the nearest or fastest-reachable hospital, ignores capability entirely. For a patient with a high-severity injury, arrival at the nearest Level-3 facility often triggers a secondary inter-facility transfer to KATH, adding 20 to 40 minutes to the effective response time and frequently requiring a second ambulance unit. The net result is worse than a direct dispatch to KATH would have been, even when KATH has a longer drive time. The routing-versus-capability trade-off is a systematic source of preventable harm, and it isn't specific to Kumasi. Emergency-medical systems in well-resourced regions encounter the same problem under load: mass-casualty events, hurricane evacuations, hospital-capacity surges. A US Gulf Coast extension of this framework, applying the same formulation to hurricane-response ambulance dispatch, is planned.

This project addresses that gap directly. We formulate ambulance dispatch as a bi-objective optimization problem over the real Kumasi road network, where the two objectives (minimize travel time, maximize capability match) are traded off explicitly. Ant Colony Optimization is our primary method; we benchmark it against four alternative approaches drawn from the metaheuristic, stochastic programming, and machine-learning literatures [1-8].

2 Problem Formulation

Let \(G = (V, E, w)\) be a directed, weighted road graph extracted from OpenStreetMap for the Kumasi Metropolitan Area, where \(V\) is the set of intersections and road endpoints, \(E \subseteq V \times V\) is the set of directed road segments, and \(w : E \to \mathbb{R}_{+}\) assigns an expected travel time to each segment based on road type and typical congestion.

Let \(H \subset V\) denote the set of hospital facility nodes, each annotated with a capability level \(\ell(h) \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) (Level 1 = highest capability). Let \(D \subset V\) be the set of potential accident-scene origins. A dispatch decision for an incoming call at origin \(d \in D\) with trauma severity \(s \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) (severity 1 = most critical) consists of selecting a hospital \(h \in H\) and routing an ambulance along a path \(r \in \mathcal{P}(d, h)\), the set of simple paths from \(d\) to \(h\) in \(G\).

The travel time along path \(r = (v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_k)\) is:

\[ T(r) = \sum_{(v_i,\, v_{i+1}) \in r} w(v_i, v_{i+1}). \]

The capability match indicator is:

\[ M(s, h) = \mathbf{1}\!\left[\ell(h) \leq s\right], \]

where \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) is 1 if the condition holds and 0 otherwise. The condition \(\ell(h) \leq s\) means the hospital's level is at least as capable as the trauma severity requires (lower level number = higher capability). A match of 1 indicates that \(h\) can handle the patient's injury category without transfer; a match of 0 means the patient would likely require onward referral.

The scalarized bi-objective dispatch problem is:

\[ \min_{h \in H,\; r \in \mathcal{P}(d,h)} \; w_1 \cdot T(r) - w_2 \cdot M(s, h), \]

where \(w_1, w_2 \geq 0\) are weights that reflect the relative importance of speed versus capability. The negative sign on the match term converts the maximization of capability match into a minimization, giving a single composite objective. The choice \(w_1 = w_2 = 1\) treats one minute of travel time and one level of capability mismatch as equally important; varying the ratio traces out the Pareto frontier between the two objectives.

In the full multi-scenario version of the problem, calls arrive stochastically and multiple ambulance units must be dispatched simultaneously. Fleet availability and hospital capacity constraints are handled through a two-stage stochastic extension described in §5.

3 The Kumasi Road Network

The road network is downloaded and constructed using OSMnx [4], which queries the OpenStreetMap Overpass API and returns a NetworkX-compatible directed multigraph. The query is bounded to the Kumasi Metropolitan Area administrative polygon. Edge weights are computed from posted speed limits combined with a road-type congestion factor calibrated to give realistic peak-hour travel times; the default OSMnx speed imputation is used as a fallback for unlabeled segments.

Hospital nodes are identified by their OSM amenity tags (amenity=hospital, amenity=clinic) and supplemented by field data to assign capability tiers. Level-1 nodes include KATH and a small number of specialist facilities; Level-2 covers district and regional hospitals; Level-3 covers community health centres and polyclinics. The augmented node attributes are stored as a GeoPackage and visualized in QGIS for manual quality control.

4 Ant Colony Optimization

ACO is a population-based metaheuristic that mimics the foraging behavior of real ant colonies to find near-optimal paths through a graph [1, 2].

In each iteration, a colony of \(m\) artificial ants independently constructs a complete dispatch assignment: selecting, for each simulated call, a hospital and a path through the road network. The construction proceeds sequentially: at each node \(i\), ant \(k\) chooses its next node \(j\) probabilistically according to two types of information: pheromone intensity \(\tau_{ij}\), which encodes the collective historical quality of paths through edge \((i,j)\), and heuristic visibility \(\eta_{ij} = 1/w_{ij}\), the inverse travel time. The state-transition rule is:

\[ p_{ij}^k = \frac{\bigl[\tau_{ij}\bigr]^\alpha \bigl[\eta_{ij}\bigr]^\beta}{\displaystyle\sum_{l \in \mathcal{N}_i^k} \bigl[\tau_{il}\bigr]^\alpha \bigl[\eta_{il}\bigr]^\beta}, \quad j \in \mathcal{N}_i^k, \]

where \(\mathcal{N}_i^k\) is the set of feasible next nodes for ant \(k\) at node \(i\), \(\alpha \geq 0\) controls the influence of pheromone (typically \(\alpha = 1\)), and \(\beta \geq 0\) controls the influence of the heuristic (typically \(\beta \in [2, 5]\)). A larger \(\alpha\) causes ants to exploit historically good paths; a larger \(\beta\) makes the search more greedy with respect to immediate travel time.

Once all ants have constructed their solutions, pheromone is updated globally. A fraction \(\rho \in (0,1)\) of the existing pheromone evaporates (preventing indefinite accumulation on suboptimal paths), and each ant deposits pheromone proportional to the quality of its solution:

\[ \tau_{ij} \leftarrow (1 - \rho)\,\tau_{ij} + \sum_{k=1}^{m} \Delta\tau_{ij}^k, \]

where:

\[ \Delta\tau_{ij}^k = \begin{cases} Q \big/ C^k & \text{if ant } k \text{ traversed edge } (i,j) \text{ in this iteration,} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Here \(C^k\) is the composite objective value of ant \(k\)'s full dispatch solution and \(Q\) is a scaling constant. Edges on better solutions receive more pheromone and become more attractive in subsequent iterations, creating a positive feedback loop that concentrates search effort on promising regions of the solution space. The evaporation term ensures that the search does not converge prematurely.

For the multi-objective extension, the composite cost \(C^k\) is computed using the scalarized objective from §2. A second run with inverted weights \((w_1, w_2)\) generates the opposite extreme of the Pareto front; intermediate weight vectors fill in the curve between the two extremes.

5 Benchmark Methods

To situate ACO's performance, four benchmark methods cover the major algorithmic families in combinatorial optimization and machine learning.

Method Paradigm Key reference
ALNS Adaptive Large Neighbourhood Search: iteratively destroys and repairs a dispatch solution using a portfolio of operators; adapts operator selection weights online Ropke & Pisinger [3]
Two-stage stochastic First-stage pre-positions ambulances; second-stage recourse handles realized demand. Explicitly models uncertainty in call locations and travel times Birge & Louveaux [6]
Deep RL Attention-based pointer network trained via REINFORCE on simulated dispatch episodes. Policy maps (call location, severity, network state) directly to dispatch decisions Nazari et al. [7]
Hybrid ACO+ML Replaces the hand-crafted heuristic \(\eta_{ij} = 1/w_{ij}\) in the ACO rule with values predicted by a graph neural network, allowing the heuristic to incorporate hospital capability alongside travel time this work

All five methods are evaluated on the same set of test scenarios generated from the Kumasi network: single-call instances (one accident, select one hospital), multi-call instances (simultaneous calls across the city), and dynamic instances (calls arrive sequentially with partial fleet availability). The primary metrics are composite objective value, gap to a Dijkstra-based lower bound, and computation time.

6 Experimental Setup

Experiments are run on the full OSMnx graph of the Kumasi Metropolitan Area. Test instances are generated by sampling demand origins uniformly from the road network and assigning trauma severities according to a categorical distribution calibrated to national Ghana road-accident injury statistics. Each method is given a fixed computational budget (wall-clock time) to allow fair comparison across algorithms with different internal iteration structures.

ACO hyperparameters (evaporation rate \(\rho\), pheromone influence \(\alpha\), heuristic influence \(\beta\), colony size \(m\), and scaling constant \(Q\)) are tuned using a grid search on a held-out validation set of instances not used in benchmarking. The same validation protocol applies to ALNS operator weights and RL policy hyperparameters.

Full benchmarking results are in preparation. This section will be expanded with comparison tables, Pareto-front plots, and an analysis of which method performs best under different network conditions (congestion levels, fleet sizes, trauma severity distributions) once the experiments complete.

7 Prior Work: KNUST Thesis (2023)

The foundation of this project is my 2023 undergraduate thesis completed in the Department of Mathematics at the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST), Kumasi, Ghana [8]. That work applied ACO to ambulance routing in the Kumasi metropolitan area, demonstrating that an ant-colony approach consistently outperformed a greedy nearest-hospital baseline on simulated dispatch scenarios.

The KNUST thesis used a single-objective formulation (minimizing expected travel time) and a simplified road graph derived from the Kumasi metropolitan area. Hospital capability was not modeled; all hospitals were treated as interchangeable end-points. The ACO implementation followed the canonical Ant System algorithm without benchmark comparison. Results showed a roughly 18 to 22% reduction in average dispatch travel time relative to the nearest-facility baseline over a set of 200 simulated calls.

The current extension addresses three limitations of the thesis: (i) the formulation is upgraded to a bi-objective problem that accounts for hospital capability, the most practically significant driver of secondary transfers; (ii) the road graph is replaced with a complete OSMnx extraction of the metropolitan area, with richer travel-time modeling; and (iii) ACO is benchmarked against a representative set of competing methods, providing empirical grounding for the claim that ACO is a competitive choice for this problem class.

The KNUST thesis is not publicly available but can be requested via the contact page.

8 Conclusions & Future Work

Ambulance routing in resource-constrained cities is not a pure distance problem. It is a capability-matching problem. Routing an ambulance to the nearest available hospital without regard to trauma severity and facility capability is a systematic source of preventable harm. This project aims to demonstrate that straightforward combinatorial optimization methods, applied to freely available road-network data, can materially improve dispatch outcomes relative to proximity-based baselines, and that ACO is competitive with more complex methods on this problem class.

Future directions: real-time traffic updates to edge weights mid-dispatch; ambulance pre-positioning and relocation between calls; extending the framework to other Ghanaian cities (Accra, Tamale) via OSMnx; and a planned US Gulf Coast extension, where the same routing-versus-capability trade-off appears under hurricane-response load and the framework transfers directly. The hybrid ACO+ML branch points toward a learning-to-optimize framework portable across urban contexts where trauma-to-facility matching is the binding constraint on outcomes.
